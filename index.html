Sources, formulas & limits

Resting energy: Mifflin-St Jeor from weight, height, age and sex. If you enter body fat, Katch-McArdle (370 + 21.6 × lean kg) is used instead. Your own resting rate per hour replaces the textbook 1 kcal/kg/hr, so exercise calories scale to you.

Exercise calories: (MET − 1) × your resting kcal/hr × hours. MET anchors: Compendium of Physical Activities (calisthenics 2.8 light, 3.8 moderate, 7.5–8.0 vigorous; resistance training 3.5–6.0) and measured push-up 3.31, pull-up 3.11, curl-up 3.14, lunge 4.05 METs (Vezina, Arizona State Univ., 2011). The MET slides with how close to failure the set was.

Afterburn (EPOC): reviews find EPOC is only 6–15% of the net exercise cost (Laforgia et al., 2006), larger with higher intensity and volume, and with rests under 60 s (Farinatti et al., 2012). I scale 6% to 15% by proximity to failure, ×1.2 for short rests. Exact resistance-exercise relationships are not established.

Heart rate (optional): Keytel et al. 2005 equations (age, weight, sex, HR). They were validated on steady aerobic exercise, and lifting raises HR out of proportion to energy use, so it gets only 30% weight in the total and is shown separately.

Load: push-up 64% of bodyweight (Ebben 2011); pull-up ≈ 100%; single-leg squat ≈ 84% (bodyweight minus free leg, Dempster); crunch ≈ 35% (my estimate, lifting the shoulder blades only). Curls and raises use your weight.

Failure proximity: Epley 1RM = load × (1 + reps/30), solved backwards; less accurate above ~12 reps.

Muscle model (v3): growth = target muscle mass × 0.03% per effective set × modifiers. The 0.03% comes from Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017: each extra weekly set added about 0.37% to percentage muscle gain (about 0.24% near 12 sets/week, with diminishing returns per Pelland et al.). I assume typical study blocks of ~10 weeks, so one set done once is about one tenth of that. Total skeletal muscle = 38.4% (men) or 30.6% (women) of body weight (Janssen et al., 2000, MRI). Share of that muscle each exercise trains (push-up 10%, pull-up 12%, crunch 3%, curl 2.5%, raise 2.5%, single-leg squat 25%) is my anatomical estimate.

Modifiers: Experience ×1 / 0.5 / 0.25 (Wernbom et al., 2007: growth rate is higher in beginners, lower after 2–3 years, small after many years; the exact numbers are mine). Sex: no modifier, because relative gains are similar in men and women (Refalo et al., 2025; Roberts et al., 2020), while absolute gains differ through muscle mass. Age: ×1 up to 30, falling to ×0.25 at 70 (Phillips et al., 2017: older adults gained 1.06% vs 4.43% in young after 20 weeks; the straight line between is my interpolation). Protein: ×0.8 at ≤0.8 g/kg rising to ×1 at 1.6 g/kg (Morton et al., 2018: supplementation added ~0.30 kg lean mass, plateau near 1.6 g/kg; scaling derived by me). Sleep: ×1 at 7 h or more, falling to ×0.81 at 4 h (Saner et al., 2020: myofibrillar protein synthesis 1.24 vs 1.53 %/day after five nights of 4 h; short-term data in young men, so extrapolation). Failure proximity: ×1 within 2 reps of failure, falling to ×0.65 at 5 (Robinson et al., 2024: growth rises as sets get closer to failure, with wide uncertainty; load matters little if sets are near failure, Refalo et al., 2023; the exact curve is mine). Volume: effective sets 1–6 per week per exercise count fully, 7–12 count ×0.65, beyond ×0.4 (from the 0.37 → 0.24 slope drop).

Honest limit: this predicts the average trend, not you. Individual response varies widely, and these numbers are only meaningful when summed over weeks of repeated training.

Not included: hormones, genetics, diet energy balance. Real calorie burn varies ±20–30%. Not medical advice.
